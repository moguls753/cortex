The other screen is the morning.

The wall holds the week. At seven in the morning a digest reads the same entries, decides what today is actually about, and lands in your inbox as an email. It is not a list dump: it picks three things, and it says out loud what has stopped moving.

It runs on a screen you already have.

The board is a PNG, fetched over HTTP. Anything that can pull an image and keep it on screen will do, and the first three of these cost nothing.

  • The phone in your pocket Open the board URL, add it to the home screen. Free, and it works before you have decided anything else.
  • An old tablet on the wall A browser in kiosk mode pointed at the same URL. Free, if the tablet is already in a drawer.
  • A browser tab on the box that is already running If something in the house is on all day anyway, the board can just live in a tab next to the dashboard.
  • A TRMNL, from about €120 Bought from TRMNL, not from me. The path with the least fiddling, and the one most people take.
  • Any bare E-Ink panel Anything that speaks the open BYOS protocol. No TRMNL account needed, and no account with me either.

E-Ink is the one this was built for. No backlight, so it does not glow at the dinner table. No microphone, so there is nothing listening in the room. And it keeps showing the last board with the power off, which is the difference between a screen and a piece of paper that updates. It is the best surface for this. It is not the requirement.

Three ways to run it.

What changes is who does the work, who is liable, and who notices when it breaks.

The dashed line is one install: one process, a database and two model containers, brought up together. It sits on your hardware or on mine, and that is the only thing the three modes below argue about. Everything on the left arrives from outside it. Everything on the right leaves again.

The two boxes that say “or an API” are where the expensive work can be handed to someone else, and both are a line in a config file, decided by what your hardware can actually carry. They are not the only things that talk to the outside world. The table further down says exactly what does.

All on your machine

Compose brings up Postgres, whisper, Ollama and the app together. It wants a real box: a NUC, an old desktop, roughly 8 GB of RAM free, because the embedding model alone is about 4.7 GB on disk. A Raspberry Pi will run the application, but it will not run the transcription.

Your machine, heavy parts on APIs you pick

The same install, with transcription and the language model pointed at a provider you choose, using your key and your billing. Your contract, not mine. This is the version that fits on a Pi.

My machine

No install, no server, no updates, and no mail server to set up so the morning digest can go out. You tap an invite link, add the bot, and start talking. The screen on the wall is still yours to choose.

What you get in the third one is not hosting. I transcribe, I sort, and I notice when it breaks.

What actually leaves the house

Every message goes through Telegram’s servers before anything of yours or mine touches it. That is true in all three modes, and it is the first thing you should know, so here is the rest of it.

What All on your machine APIs you pick My machine
The audio Telegram, then your box. Telegram, then your box, then the transcription provider you named. Telegram, then my box.
The text Your box, and no further. Your box, plus the model provider you named. My box, plus the model provider I name below.
The board image Your box, pulled by whatever screen you pointed at it. Your box, pulled by whatever screen you pointed at it. My box, pulled by whatever screen you pointed at it.
The weather open-meteo is called on every render, in every mode. It gets a coordinate and gives back a forecast.
Your calendar Google, in every mode, but only if you connect a calendar. Connect nothing and nothing is called.

So “nothing leaves your house” would be a lie, in all three modes, and you would find that out in ten minutes with a packet capture. What local transcription is actually worth is narrower and still worth having: the recording is never handed to a second company, and the text only goes where you pointed it.

Two doors, for two different people.

No feature is gated, no licence is checked, and there is no code path that behaves differently depending on who runs it. Same repository, same AGPL-3.0, both sides. What differs is what you get and what you give.

You run it

Nothing of yours reaches me at all. Not the audio, not the text, not a crash report.

And the part that gets missed: one person running a box for their own household sits inside the GDPR household exemption, Article 2(2)(c). Nobody is anybody’s data controller. There is no processing agreement, no register of activities, nothing to sign, because none of it applies to you.

The usual objection is “so now I need an API subscription too”. Barely. Call it twenty messages a day, six hundred a month, about 700 tokens in and 200 out each. On a small hosted model that is three to five cents a month for the whole household. Transcription is the part that costs real money, and transcription is exactly the part you can keep local.

You give: a box, power, and attention.

I run it

It runs without you. Updates, migrations, the model that quietly changed its output format overnight.

And the honest one, which argues against the column you are reading: a self-hosted setup fails silently. The bot stops answering, the panel shows Tuesday for three days, and you do not notice, because this is precisely the thing you never open. There is no error message, because nothing is watching for one.

That is what the money is for. Someone notices, and it is fixed before you would have found it. It is the same product either way, so the only question is whether you want that job.

You give: money.

What I do with what reaches me, stated plainly.

Encrypted in transit and encrypted at rest. Your entries never train a model and are never sold or passed on for anyone else’s purposes. There are four subprocessors and they are named: Telegram for delivery, Hetzner in Germany for the machines, open-meteo for the weather, and Anthropic for the structuring step.

Transcription runs on my own machines, which is the whole point of doing it that way: the recording is not handed on to a second company, and it is discarded once the text exists. Every entry is exportable and every entry is deletable, including all of them at once, including the account.

Where it will let you down.

Does this

  • Telegram in, text or voice
  • Transcription, on your box or on mine
  • Structuring into appointments, tasks and notes
  • The board, served over BYOS to whatever screen you point at it
  • The morning digest, sent as an email at seven
  • An MCP server, so an assistant can read and write the same entries

Rough

  • Self-hosting is Docker, an .env file and a wizard. Nothing about it is one click.
  • Self-hosting the digest means giving it a mail server to send from. That is the step that surprises people.
  • English and German only.
  • The dashboard exists, but it is not the point, and it looks like it.

Open

  • How well it structures a sentence it has never seen before. That one is below, at length.
  • Whether a panel on the wall really beats the phone already in your hand.
  • Whether speaking is what people want at all. That is what the question at the top is for.

The accuracy of the structuring step is structurally unmeasurable, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

There is no ground truth for “this thought was filed correctly”. No public benchmark covers it, and a set I label myself only measures my agreement with my own judgement on my own messages. So there is no percentage on this page. Anyone quoting you a number for this class of task is guessing, including me.

What matters more is what happens when it gets one wrong, because it will. It puts the dentist on the wrong Thursday. It files something as a note that you meant as a task. The sentence you actually said is kept beside whatever it was turned into, so you can see what it did and change it, in the dashboard or by telling the bot. A wrong call costs you ten seconds. It does not cost you the thought, and that is the part I would not compromise on.

What this is not

Echo Show has done voice at the wall for years, at €329, and does it well. Saying nobody else does this would be a lie. The difference here is narrow, and worth stating exactly: you speak one free-form thought instead of a list command, something structures it instead of filing it verbatim, it runs on hardware you picked, and the surface is E-Ink that sits still and does not ask for anything.

The neighbours

Wall tablets like Dæly, Hearth and Skylight expect somebody to type. TRMNL has no input at all, by design. hass-eink-dashboard renders entities you have already filled in somewhere else. None of them are bad. They just answer a different question than “what do I do with the thought I had on the stairs”.

Stack

  • TypeScript on Node, Hono
  • grammy for the Telegram bot
  • Drizzle over PostgreSQL with pgvector
  • node-cron for the digest, SMTP to deliver it

Models

  • faster-whisper for voice, in its own container
  • Embeddings via Ollama: qwen3-embedding, about 4.7 GB on disk, 4096 dimensions
  • Structuring is LLM-agnostic: Anthropic, OpenAI, or any compatible endpoint

Display

  • Satori to SVG, resvg to PNG
  • Greyscale, sized to the panel you point at it
  • Served over BYOS, so any client that can fetch it works
  • Seven MCP tools over the same data
