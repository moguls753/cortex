Where it runs

Three ways. What changes is who does the work, who is liable, and who notices when it breaks.

The same program in all three. The same repository, the same database, the same board on the same wall. Nothing is switched off in one of them to make another one worth paying for.

All on your machine

One compose file brings up Postgres, Whisper, Ollama and the app together. It wants a real box — a NUC, an old desktop, something with about 8 GB of RAM to spare. The embedding model alone is roughly 4.7 GB on disk and has to be in memory while it works; the transcription model wants its own room next to it.

A Raspberry Pi will run the application quite happily. It will not run the transcription.

Your machine, the heavy parts on APIs you pick

The same install, with transcription and the language model pointed at a provider you choose, on your key, on your account. If the provider changes its terms, raises its price or goes away, that is a conversation you have with them.

Your contract, not mine.

This one fits on a Pi.

My machine

No install, no server, no updates, no mail server to configure. You tap an invite link, speak into the same bot, and the panel on your wall fetches its board from my renderer instead of yours.

Second mode, first objection: so now I need an API subscription as well. The sorting is smaller than it sounds. A busy household puts in maybe 300 things a month, and each one is roughly 800 tokens in and out including the context it gets shown — call it a quarter of a million tokens. At what the small models cost today, ten to twenty cents per million, that is three to five cents a month. Transcription is the part with a real bill on it, and in the first mode it has none: Whisper runs on your own box for the price of the electricity.

I transcribe, I sort, and I notice when it breaks.

The third mode is the one you can pay for, and that sentence is what it is. Not “hosting” — hosting is a word for renting a machine, and a machine is the part you already have. What it buys is the two jobs weak hardware cannot do, and one that is not a machine job at all.

Honest status

Voice at the wall is not new.

Echo Show has done it for years, at €329, and pretending otherwise would be the fastest way to lose the only audience this page is written for. The difference here is narrow, and it is worth saying at exactly its real size: you speak a free-form thought rather than a list command, something actually structures it instead of appending a string to a list, the processing can happen entirely on your own machine, and it lands on E-Ink: a surface that is simply on, with no backlight, no camera and no microphone in the room.

That is the entire claim. The rest of the wall-display field is a different shape: Dæly, Hearth and Skylight want you to type on a tablet on the wall, TRMNL has no input path at all, and hass-eink-dashboard renders entities you must already have populated by hand.

What it does

  • Text and voice in through a Telegram bot
  • Transcription on your own machine, or an API you pick, or mine
  • An LLM does the structuring, into five categories
  • Every reply carries those five as buttons, so a wrong call is one tap to move
  • Board rendered server-side, Satori to resvg, 4-bit greyscale
  • Pushed over BYOS to the panel
  • The morning digest, by email
  • An MCP server and search over what you have said

What it does not do

  • No app of its own. Telegram is the way in, on purpose: it is already on the phone and it already does voice
  • The panel cannot be typed on. It shows; it never asks
  • The digest is email, not chat. Self-hosted, that means giving it an SMTP server to send through — the least charming hour of the setup
  • It does not keep everything indoors. What leaves, and in which mode, is listed one block up — starting with the audio, which reaches Telegram before it reaches anything of yours or mine
  • “crtxboard” is not reliably spellable from hearing it out loud. Known, accepted, links carry it
  • No photograph of my home anywhere on this page. The wall scene near the top is composed: the panel and the board on it are real, the room is not
  • No users to point at, no testimonials, no case studies. There are none, so there are none on this page

Accuracy is currently structurally unmeasurable.

How often the structuring step gets it right is not something I can put a number on today, and the reason is not laziness. There is nothing to score against. Whether “don't forget the bins” belongs on the wall, in a reminder for tonight, or nowhere at all depends on the household, not on the sentence. Build a test set and you have written down one household's answers and called them ground truth.

So there is no accuracy figure anywhere on this page, and there will not be one until there is a way to measure it that would mean something. If that is a dealbreaker for you, it should be, and it is a reason to answer the question at the top with the second option. That is exactly the signal this page exists to collect.

And when it gets one wrong.

It looks like this: the dentist appointment lands under Don't forget instead of under Thursday. It is on the wall either way, in the wrong half of it.

Two things keep that from being expensive. The thing you said is stored whether or not the sorting succeeds — a bad call misfiles a line, it never loses one. And the bot's reply comes back with the five categories as buttons underneath it, so moving it is one tap in the chat you already have open, and it stays where you put it.

That is the whole recovery story, and it is why an unmeasurable accuracy figure is survivable rather than fatal: the cost of a wrong guess is one tap, paid by the person who noticed.
