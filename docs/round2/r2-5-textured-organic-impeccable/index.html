Voice at the wall is not new.

Echo Show has done it for years, at €329, and it does it well. Anyone telling you nobody has solved this is selling you something. The difference here is narrower than a marketing page would like it to be, so here it is at actual size:

  • You speak a free-form thought, not a list command. No wake phrase grammar, no “add milk to shopping list”.
  • Something structures it — a date, a person, a thing not to forget, instead of appending a line to a list.
  • The transcription can run on your own machine, so the recording is never handed to a second company after Telegram has had it. Exactly what leaves is listed further down, mode by mode.
  • The surface is E-Ink. The panel on the wall is a display and nothing else: no backlight, no camera, no microphone in it.

That is the whole claim. If none of those four are worth anything to you, the honest answer above is “I’d type it in myself”, and that is a genuinely useful thing for me to learn.

The board is a PNG, fetched over HTTP on a timer — so anything that can pull an image can show it. The free options are listed first on purpose: you should not have to buy an object to find out whether you want this at all.

BYOS is the open protocol the panels speak, and it means the device asks your server for its next picture. No vendor account sits in the middle, TRMNL’s included. Nothing on this page ships you an object either — no bundle, no kit, no pre-order.

E-Ink is still the version this was built for. No backlight glowing at you across a dark kitchen, no microphone added to the room, and it keeps showing the last board with the power off. The best surface for it — not the requirement.

Three ways to run it.

The same code in all three — same repository, same container, same licence. What changes is who does the work, who is liable, and who notices when it breaks.

01 · yours

All on your machine

One compose file brings up Postgres, Whisper, a local model and the app. It wants a real box: a NUC, an old desktop, a NAS with room to spare.

  • needsabout 8 GB of RAM free — the embedding model alone is 4.7 GB
  • a piruns the application happily, and the transcription not at all
  • mailan SMTP server of your own, because the 06:30 briefing is an email
02 · yours, mostly

Your machine, heavy parts on APIs you pick

The same installation, with transcription and the language model pointed at a provider you choose, on your own key and your own account. Your contract, not mine. This one fits on a Pi.

  • needsa Raspberry Pi is enough
  • keysyours — I never see the traffic or the bill
  • costabout €0.03–0.05 per household per month, for the sorting
  • mailstill your own SMTP server — the briefing is an email in every mode

Since somebody always asks whether this means another subscription, the workings: fifteen messages a day is roughly 450 classifications a month at ~500 tokens each — a quarter of a million tokens. At the rates the usual providers list for their small models, ~€0.10–0.15 per million, that is three to five cents.

03 · mine

My machine

No installation, no server, no updates, no compose file. Tap the invite link, say something to the bot, and point whatever screen you own at the board address it hands back.

  • needsa Telegram account, and a screen from the shelf above
  • updatesmine to do — and mine to break
  • modelsmy machines, my keys, my bill
  • leavingexport it and move onto your own box, without asking me

I transcribe, I sort, and I notice when it breaks.

That is what the third column actually sells, and it is worth being precise about it: not “hosting”. Two of those three are the parts that weak hardware does badly, and the third is not a machine at all.

And the honest thing on this side: a self-hosted setup fails silently. The bot stops answering, the panel shows Tuesday for three days, and you never find out — because this is precisely the thing you never open. Someone whose job it is to notice is the whole difference.

Nothing is gated between the two. No licence check, no telemetry, no feature held back, no code path that behaves differently depending on who is running it. They are two doors into the same room, built for different people, and I would rather say plainly which is which than pretend one of them is the proper way.

What the hosted one costs is at the foot of this page, on its own.

Local transcription is still worth having, and the first column is exactly what it buys: after Telegram, the recording reaches your box and stops there. Point transcription at an API instead and it reaches one company more — the one you chose, on your key. That is a smaller claim than “fully local”. It is also the true one, and it is checkable in src/telegram.ts in about a minute.

If I am the one running it

  • Encrypted in transit, and encrypted at rest.
  • Your entries never train anything — not a model of mine, not anyone else’s.
  • Nothing is sold, rented or passed to an advertiser. There is no second business model here.
  • Every subprocessor is named in the privacy notice — the machine, the mail relay, the model provider — not filed under “trusted partners”.
  • Delete an entry and it goes. Close the account and the rest goes with it, backups included once they rotate out.
  • The audio is discarded once it has been transcribed. What is kept is the text.

What it does, what it won’t, and what nobody has measured.

One person, one kitchen, one panel on a fridge door. The interesting part of a thing like this is the part that is still open, so it is on the page rather than in a footnote.

What it does
  • Telegram in: typed messages and voice messages
  • Transcription, on your own machine if you want it there
  • A language model turns the sentence into entries
  • Wall render: Satori → resvg, 4-bit greyscale
  • The 06:30 briefing, by email
  • Any BYOS device, any browser, any old tablet
  • A small web view, for the times you do want to go and look
What it will not do
  • Nothing to tap on the wall. The panel is a display, with no microphone in it
  • No phone app. Telegram is the app, on purpose
  • One household per installation, not a workspace with roles and permissions
  • It controls nothing: no lights, no heating, no scenes
  • It cannot flag the case that actually costs you: confident, and wrong
Not measured
  • How often the structuring step gets it right
  • How often it silently drops half a sentence
  • How it behaves on accents and dialect
  • Whether anybody keeps using it after week three
  • No benchmark exists for any of these, and I will not invent one

Accuracy is currently structurally unmeasurable.

Not “we haven’t got round to it”. There is no ground truth to measure against. Doing it properly means sitting down and writing out, by hand, what the correct structured result should have been for a few hundred real messages. Real messages are, by definition, my own private ones. Nobody else’s are available to me, which is rather the point of the product.

So any percentage on this page about classification quality would be a number I invented. There isn’t one here, and there isn’t one hiding in a footnote either. If that is a dealbreaker for you, it should be. The question at the top of the page is exactly where to say so.

What matters more than the missing number is what happens when it is wrong. It replies with what it filed and how sure it was, as a percentage; when it is not sure, it says so in the chat and offers five category buttons to put it right in one tap. /fix put that on Thursday redoes the last entry, and any entry can be edited or deleted in the web view, with a bin so that a wrong deletion is not final.

The expensive case is the other one — confident and wrong. Nothing flags that, by definition, and the wall goes on showing it until somebody notices. That is the failure mode to expect, and it is the reason the question at the top of the page is the honest one to ask.
